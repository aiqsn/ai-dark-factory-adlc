What is a dark factory?

Definition

A dark factory is a software delivery system in which AI agents do the repetitive work of turning written specifications into tested, merged and deployed software, without a person typing or reading each line of code. People supply what the agents cannot: the judgement about what is worth building, the definition of what good looks like, and the decisions on anything risky or unclear. The lights are off on the production line, not on the people who direct it.

In one line: people decide what good looks like; agents build to that standard around the clock.

Purpose

A dark factory exists so that delivery is limited by how clearly you can specify and judge work, not by how many engineer-hours you have.

  • Continuous output. Work runs unattended, overnight and in parallel, instead of waiting for someone to write or review it.
  • Human judgement at the centre. People decide what to build, set the bar for "good", and make the calls agents can't. Agents carry that judgement out at scale; they don't replace it.
  • Quality that scales. Gates and hidden scenarios check every change the same way, every time; reviewer attention does neither.

The rest of this section explains where the idea comes from, how teams run one today, and its limits.

Where the name comes from. Manufacturing has "lights-out" factories, such as FANUC's plant where robots build robots without people on the floor. They can run in the dark because robots don't need to see. In January 2026, Dan Shapiro borrowed the term for the top rung of his five levels of AI-assisted programming, a scale modelled on the levels of self-driving cars. In his words, at Level 5 "it's not really a car any more": it is "a black box that turns specs into software" (Simon Willison's summary).

The levels describe how the human job changes, not how good the AI is. At each step up, people type and read less code themselves and put more of their expertise into the standards the code is held to. At Level 4 they write specs and agents build. At Level 5 they judge outcomes instead of lines of code, so the quality of their judgement sets the quality of the software.

How a real one works. The best-documented example is StrongDM's Software Factory, run by a team of three. Its charter has two rules: code must not be written by humans, and code must not be reviewed by humans. Those rules don't take people out of the work: they move the team's expertise into the specs and scenarios that every change is measured against. Four ideas make that workable:

  • Specs are the source of truth. Humans write detailed specifications; agents turn them into code and keep iterating until it converges.
  • Scenarios, not just tests. Instead of pass/fail unit tests, the team writes step-by-step stories of how a real customer would use the product, and asks how often the software actually does what that customer needed.
  • Hidden scenarios. Those scenarios are kept away from the coding agents, so agents can't learn the answers. This matters: StrongDM's agents once wrote return true, which passes any test and does nothing useful.
  • Digital twins. Working replicas of outside services (Okta, Jira, Slack, Google Docs, Drive and Sheets) let thousands of scenarios run per hour with no rate limits, API costs or risk to real systems.

Success is a satisfaction score judged by AI, and the team treats compute as cheap: its CTO suggests that under $1,000 of tokens per engineer per day means "your software factory has room for improvement."

What it is not. A dark factory is not "AI writes code and nobody checks." The checking moves from reading diffs to designing the specs, scenarios and gates that judge results. Those gates are only as good as the people who design them: every scenario, rubric and threshold records a person's view of what good looks like. The open risks are real, and this guide's guardrails exist because of them:

  • Gaming the score: agents optimise for whatever is measured, which is why the scenarios must stay hidden.
  • Circular judging: when the same kind of model builds and grades the work, shared blind spots can slip through.
  • Accountability: if no person reviewed the code, it is harder to say who answers for a failure.
  • Fading skills: engineers who stop reading code may lose the ability to diagnose it when something breaks badly.

What stays human. The factory automates the build-and-check loop, not the judgement that steers it. Good judgement and the definition of good still come from people:

  • What is worth building, and why. People choose the work and write the spec with AI. An agent can draft a spec; it can't decide what matters to your users.
  • What good looks like. Acceptance criteria, hidden scenarios, review rubrics, probe checks and alert thresholds are all written by people. The agents are held to that standard; they don't set it.
  • The hard calls. Every needs-human escalation, every risky change and every rollback that needs a decision goes to a person.
  • How far to trust the factory. Each step of the rollout is a person's decision, made from the metrics.
  • Accountability. A named person owns each standard the factory uses and answers for what it ships.

What you are building

You can run a working dark-factory loop for $0 a month: a public GitHub repo, GitHub Actions, the open-source aider agent and free model tiers from OpenRouter and others. A one-time $10 OpenRouter top-up is the only spend worth making, because it lifts the free-model cap from 50 to 1,000 requests a day for good.

The ADLC (agentic development lifecycle) here has six stages, each a GitHub primitive:

  1. Spec — a person writes the issue with AI from a template, including acceptance criteria, and approves it.
  2. Build — a label triggers an Actions job where aider implements the spec and loops on tests.
  3. Verify — the PR runs unit tests, then hidden holdout scenarios the agent never saw.
  4. Review — two adversarial reviewers from different model families try to reject the diff, while probes and mutation testing attack the tests.
  5. Merge or escalate — green and low-risk PRs auto-merge; everything else is labelled for a human.
  6. Deploy — each merge goes out in stages (preview, a 10% canary, then 100%) behind live probes, and rolls itself back if any probe fails.

People stay in charge of the judgement: what to build, what good looks like, and what to do when a gate can't decide. Their time moves from typing code to writing specs with AI, curating the hidden scenarios that define quality, deciding escalations and tuning the factory itself.

How the pipeline fits together

The build-and-check loop runs on its own, but it runs on human judgement. People write and approve the spec, write the hidden scenarios the agent never reads, set the merge policy, and decide every escalation.

Wiring the extra keys into the build job. Aider reaches all of these through LiteLLM provider prefixes (gemini/…, groq/…, mistral/…) or any OpenAI-compatible endpoint via --openai-api-base. Replace the single-model loop with a chain held in one variable, so changing the order or dropping a provider is a settings edit, not a code change. A provider whose secret is missing or whose quota is spent simply fails and the loop moves on.

              --yes-always --no-auto-commits --no-gitignore \
              --no-show-model-warnings --map-tokens 1024 \

Setup, step by step

Setup takes about an hour and touches three places: your model provider consoles, the factory repo and a private scenarios repo.

  1. Model provider keys. Create one key per provider you plan to use (table below). OpenRouter is the only required one. Set a low spend or credit limit wherever the console offers it, and optionally buy $10 of OpenRouter credit once to unlock 1,000 free requests a day.
  2. Factory repo (public). Add a Makefile with a test target that exits non-zero on failure. Everything in the pipeline calls make test, so the language does not matter.
  3. AGENTS.md. The factory's standing instructions: stack, code style, how to run tests, and hard rules (never touch .github/, scenarios/ or AGENTS.md; never delete tests; keep changes under ~300 lines).
  4. Spec template. Add .github/ISSUE_TEMPLATE/spec.md with four headings: Goal, Acceptance criteria (as checkable bullets), Out of scope, Files likely touched. Bad specs are the main cause of bad output at this level. Draft each spec with an AI chat assistant: give it the template and a rough idea, ask it for acceptance criteria and edge cases, then edit the result yourself before filing it. Worked examples follow in the next section.
  5. Scenarios repo (private). Create <you>/<app>-scenarios holding end-to-end checks run against the built app, plus a run.sh entry point. The implementer never sees this repo.
  6. Tokens. Create two fine-grained personal access tokens:
    • FACTORY_PAT: factory repo only; Contents, Pull requests and Issues read/write. Needed because PRs opened with the default GITHUB_TOKEN do not trigger other workflows, so CI would never run.
    • SCENARIOS_TOKEN: scenarios repo only; Contents read.
  7. Secrets and variables (Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions):
    • Required secrets: OPENROUTER_API_KEY, FACTORY_PAT, SCENARIOS_TOKEN.
    • Optional secrets, one per extra provider: GEMINI_API_KEY, GROQ_API_KEY, MISTRAL_API_KEY, REQUESTY_API_KEY, CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN with CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID. GitHub Models and Ollama need none. For alerts, optionally DISCORD_WEBHOOK and FACTORY_ADMIN_PAT (lets critical alerts pause the factory). For deploys, CLOUDFLARE_DEPLOY_TOKEN and PROBE_API_KEY; the AWS version signs in with OIDC and needs no AWS secret.
    • Variables: FACTORY_MODEL and FACTORY_FALLBACK_MODEL for the single-provider workflow, or FACTORY_MODELS for the multi-provider chain; REVIEW_MODEL, PROSECUTOR_MODEL and SPEC_REVIEW_MODEL for the adversarial reviews; FACTORY_ENABLED=true as a kill switch; optionally APP_URL for the nightly holdouts and ALERT_ASSIGNEE for who gets alerts; for deploys, WORKER_NAME, or LAMBDA_FUNCTION, CANDIDATE_URL, AWS_DEPLOY_ROLE_ARN and AWS_REGION.
  8. Labels. spec (marks spec issues for red-teaming), factory:build (starts a run), factory (marks agent PRs), needs-human (escalation), factory-alert (the health check's alert issue), escaped-bug (a bug found in auto-merged code).
  9. Repo settings. Turn on Allow auto-merge. Add a ruleset on main: require a pull request, require the factory-verify / verify status check, block force pushes. Create a production environment for deploys (see Deploying with probes and automatic rollback).
  10. Dry run. File a trivial spec ("add a --version flag"), apply factory:build, and watch the Actions tab end to end before trusting it with anything real.

Writing specs, with examples

The spec is where a person's judgement enters the factory: what to build, why, and what good looks like. Its quality caps everything downstream. A buildable spec has one outcome, criteria a test can check, and a clear fence around what the agent must not touch. You write it with AI, then edit and approve it yourself.

The template

Save this as .github/ISSUE_TEMPLATE/spec.md. The front matter adds the spec label, which triggers the spec red-team.

markdown
---
name: Factory spec
about: One unit of work for the dark factory
labels: spec
---
## Goal
One or two sentences: the user-visible outcome, and why it matters.

## Acceptance criteria
- [ ] Each criterion is observable and checkable by an automated test.
- [ ] Name exact inputs, outputs, status codes and messages.
- [ ] Include at least one error or edge case.

## Out of scope
- What the agent must not change, add or "improve".

## Files likely touched
- path/to/file.py

Drafting a spec with AI

Paste this into any AI chat assistant, fill in the brackets, and answer its questions before it writes. Then edit the draft yourself. You own the result.

text
Help me write a spec for an autonomous coding agent. Use this template:
<paste the template>

My idea: <one or two sentences>
Context: <stack, relevant files, constraints, anything already decided>

Before writing, ask me up to five questions about anything ambiguous.
Then write the spec. Rules:
- One outcome. If it needs "and", propose splitting it into two specs.
- Every acceptance criterion must be checkable by an automated test and name exact
  inputs, outputs and errors.
- Include at least one error case and one edge case.
- List out-of-scope items explicitly, including "no new dependencies" if that applies.
- Keep the change small enough for about 300 lines.

Example 1: from weak to buildable

The weak version. This is how most first drafts look.

markdown
## Goal
Add rate limiting to the API so it doesn't get abused.

What the spec red-team said. This is the kind of comment the red-team workflow posts on that issue.

markdown
- [ ] "Abused" is undefined: per API key, per IP, or global? What limit, over what window?
- [ ] No behaviour when the limit is hit: status code, response body, Retry-After header?
- [ ] Untestable: "doesn't get abused". Testable rewrite: "the 61st request within
      60 seconds from one API key returns 429".
- [ ] Missing edge cases: requests without a key, admin or internal callers.
- [ ] Needs a decision: which endpoints are covered, and are limits configurable?

The buildable version, after answering those questions:

markdown
## Goal
Stop a single API key from flooding /api/search, which slows search for everyone.

## Acceptance criteria
- [ ] Each API key may make 60 requests to GET /api/search per fixed 60-second window.
- [ ] The 61st request in a window returns HTTP 429 with body
      {"error": "rate_limited", "retry_after": <seconds>} and a Retry-After header
      giving the same number of seconds.
- [ ] Requests without an API key are limited to 20 per window per client IP.
- [ ] Keys with role=admin are never limited.
- [ ] Limits come from SEARCH_RATE_LIMIT and SEARCH_ANON_RATE_LIMIT (defaults 60 and 20).
- [ ] New tests cover: 60th request succeeds, 61st returns 429, the window resets,
      admin keys are exempt, and the anonymous limit. Existing search tests still pass.

## Out of scope
- Any endpoint other than /api/search.
- Limiting across multiple app instances; an in-memory store is fine.
- New dependencies, including Redis.

## Files likely touched
- src/middleware/ratelimit.py (new)
- src/api/search.py
- tests/test_ratelimit.py (new)

Example 2: a bug fix

Bugs are specs too. Give the reproduction, the expected and actual results, and require a regression test that fails on the current code.

markdown
## Goal
Report rows show the wrong date for users west of UTC: an event on the evening of
10 March appears under 9 March. Dates must show the calendar day in the user's own time zone.

## Steps to reproduce
1. Set a user's time zone to America/Los_Angeles.
2. Create an event at 2026-03-10T17:00:00Z (10:00 local time on 10 March).
3. Open the weekly report. Expected: 2026-03-10. Actual: 2026-03-09.

## Acceptance criteria
- [ ] 2026-03-10T17:00:00Z viewed in America/Los_Angeles shows 2026-03-10.
- [ ] 2026-03-10T06:00:00Z viewed in America/Los_Angeles shows 2026-03-09
      (23:00 on 9 March, local time).
- [ ] Daylight saving edge: 2026-03-08T10:30:00Z shows 2026-03-08 in America/Los_Angeles.
- [ ] Users with no time zone set still see UTC dates.
- [ ] A regression test reproduces the original bug and fails on the current main branch.

## Out of scope
- Stored timestamps stay in UTC; do not migrate data.
- CSV and PDF exports (a separate spec).

## Files likely touched
- src/reports/format.py
- tests/reports/test_dates.py

Example 3: a hidden holdout scenario

The holdout for Example 1 lives in the private scenarios repo, where the agent never sees it. It tests the same promise the way a real partner would hit it, rather than repeating the acceptance criteria.

python
# scenarios/test_search_partner_burst.py  (private repo, run by run.sh)
import os, time, requests

URL = os.environ["APP_URL"] + "/api/search"
KEY = {"X-API-Key": "partner-burst-test"}

def search():
    return requests.get(URL, params={"q": "running shoes"}, headers=KEY, timeout=5)

def test_partner_burst_is_limited_then_recovers():
    codes = [search().status_code for _ in range(75)]
    assert codes[:60] == [200] * 60, "a normal burst must be served"
    assert set(codes[60:]) == {429}, "the excess must be rate limited"

    retry_after = int(search().headers["Retry-After"])
    assert 1 <= retry_after <= 60

    time.sleep(retry_after + 1)
    recovered = search()
    assert recovered.status_code == 200
    assert recovered.elapsed.total_seconds() < 0.5, "search must stay fast after a burst"

Spec mistakes that break factories

Mistake Example Fix
Vague adjective "Make search fast" "p95 latency under 200 ms on the 10,000-row fixture"
Two outcomes in one "Add CSV export and redesign the report page" Split into two specs; each gets its own PR
No failure behaviour "Users can upload an avatar" Name the size and type limits, and the error each one returns
Criterion restates the goal "Rate limiting works correctly" State the input, the expected output and the boundary
Hidden assumption "Use the usual auth" Name the mechanism and the file it lives in
No fence Nothing under Out of scope List what must not change, including "no new dependencies"

The workflow files

Three workflows carry a spec to users: factory-build turns a labelled issue into a PR, factory-verify tests, reviews and merges or escalates it, and deploy ships each merge in stages (it's covered in full under Deploying with probes and automatic rollback). Issue text reaches the shell only through env:, never as ${{ }} inside run:, which closes the classic Actions script-injection hole.

1. Build: issue → PR

yaml
# .github/workflows/factory-build.yml
name: factory-build
on:
  issues:
    types: [labeled]

permissions:
  contents: read

concurrency:
  group: factory-build          # one build at a time
  cancel-in-progress: false

jobs:
  build:
    if: github.event.label.name == 'factory:build' && vars.FACTORY_ENABLED == 'true'
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    timeout-minutes: 30
    env:
      OPENROUTER_API_KEY: ${{ secrets.OPENROUTER_API_KEY }}
      GH_TOKEN: ${{ secrets.FACTORY_PAT }}
      ISSUE: ${{ github.event.issue.number }}
      TITLE: ${{ github.event.issue.title }}
      BODY: ${{ github.event.issue.body }}
      MODEL: ${{ vars.FACTORY_MODEL }}
      FALLBACK: ${{ vars.FACTORY_FALLBACK_MODEL }}
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
        with:
          token: ${{ secrets.FACTORY_PAT }}
          fetch-depth: 0

3. Reviewer rubric

The rubric lives under .github/, so the implementer cannot edit the rules it is judged by.

markdown
# .github/factory/review-rubric.md
You review pull requests written by an autonomous coding agent.
Reject (approve=false) if any of these hold:
- Tests were deleted, skipped, or weakened.
- Code special-cases test inputs or returns hard-coded values to pass.
- The diff goes beyond the linked spec or touches auth, secrets, CI, or dependencies.
- New dependencies were added without being named in the spec.
Risk: low = isolated change with tests; medium = shared code paths; high = data, auth, or public API.
Be terse. Output JSON only.

If the reviewer returns anything unparseable, the merge step treats it as approve=false, risk=high and escalates, so a flaky free model fails safe.

Adversarial reviews

A single friendly reviewer is the weakest gate in the factory: it shares the builder's blind spots and is easy to satisfy. So every check here is adversarial. Reviewers are told to find a reason to reject, they come from different model families, and the tests themselves get attacked to prove they can catch a bug.

Check What it attacks When Free-tier cost
Spec red-team The spec: ambiguous words, untestable criteria, missing edge cases When a spec issue is opened, before any build 1 Gemini Flash-Lite request per spec
Reward-hacking probes The diff: deleted test files, new skips, a net loss of assertions Every PR Runner minutes only
Mutation testing The tests: small bugs are planted in the changed code; tests that still pass are too weak Every PR, on changed files only Runner minutes only
Prosecutor review The code: assumes it is wrong and must cite a file and line for every defect Every PR 1 GitHub Models request
Second-family review The prosecutor's blind spots: an independent model from a different family, using the standard rubric Every PR, in parallel 1 OpenRouter free request
Scenario adversary The holdouts: proposes new hidden scenarios from escaped bugs and recent failures Weekly A few requests, in the scenarios repo

Rules that keep the reviews independent:

  • Spec and diff only. Reviewers never see the builder's transcript or reasoning, which would anchor them on its story.
  • Different families. The implementer, the prosecutor and the second reviewer should all come from different model families.
  • Blind and parallel. Both reviewers run at the same time, and neither sees the other's verdict.
  • Unanimous to merge. Auto-merge needs every check to pass and both reviewers to approve at low risk. Any reject goes to needs-human.
  • Evidence or it doesn't count. A defect must name a file, a line and an input that breaks it. That keeps the prosecutor from rejecting everything on vague grounds.
  • Watch the reviewers too. Each week, track how often the two reviewers disagree, how many rejects a person overturns, the mutation score, and escaped bugs. Tighten whichever is drifting.

probes.sh fails the PR when the diff looks like it games the tests. These checks are deterministic, so no model can talk its way past them.

bash
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# .github/factory/probes.sh <base-ref>
range="$1...HEAD"; fail=0
if git diff --name-status "$range" | awk '$1 ~ /^D/ {print $2}' | grep -E '(^|/)(tests?|spec|__tests__)/'; then
  echo "::error::Test files were deleted"; fail=1
fi
if git diff "$range" | grep -E '^\+' | grep -E 'pytest\.mark\.skip|pytest\.skip\(|\bxit\(|\bxdescribe\(|\.skip\(|@Ignore|@Disabled|t\.Skip\(|#\[ignore\]'; then
  echo "::error::Tests were newly skipped"; fail=1
fi
removed=$(git diff "$range" -- '*test*' | grep -cE '^-[^-].*(assert|expect\()' || true)
added=$(git diff "$range" -- '*test*' | grep -cE '^\+[^+].*(assert|expect\()' || true)
if [ "$removed" -gt "$added" ]; then
  echo "::error::Net loss of assertions ($removed removed, $added added)"; fail=1
fi
exit $fail

Mutation testing. Define a make mutation target that mutates only the files changed since $(BASE) and fails when too many mutants survive. Start the threshold low and raise it as the suite improves. Common free tools: mutmut for Python, Stryker for JavaScript, TypeScript and C#, cargo-mutants for Rust (its --in-diff option limits it to a diff), and PIT for Java. Mutation runs are slow, so give the step a timeout and keep it to changed files.

What a good probe looks like

Probes are a short, fast, read-only check of the paths users depend on. They are not your test suite: the full tests already ran in CI, and the hidden holdouts already ran on the PR.

  • Read-only. Probes run against production, so they must never create, change or delete data. Give them a dedicated PROBE_API_KEY with read-only access.
  • Know which version answered. The app exposes /version, which returns the deployed git SHA. Otherwise a probe could pass by hitting the old version.
  • Critical paths only. Five to fifteen checks: health, the main page, the busiest API calls, and one expected error.
  • Repeat and time them. Each check runs three times, and any response slower than MAX_SECONDS (default 1.5) fails.
  • Same probes everywhere. They run against the new version before it takes traffic, during the canary, in production after promotion, and every night from the health check.

Your app needs two small endpoints. In a Worker, for example:

js
// inside your Worker's fetch handler
if (url.pathname === "/healthz") return new Response("ok");
if (url.pathname === "/version") return new Response(env.GIT_SHA ?? "unknown");

List the checks in probes/checks.txt, one per line:

text
# METHOD  PATH                   STATUS  TEXT THAT MUST APPEAR (optional)
GET       /healthz               200     ok
GET       /api/search?q=shoes    200     "results"
GET       /api/search            400     "missing q"
GET       /does-not-exist        404

smoke.sh runs them and writes a results table to the job summary. It exits non-zero on any wrong status, missing text, slow response or wrong version.

bash
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# .github/factory/smoke.sh <base-url> [expected-git-sha] [extra request header]
# Read-only probes: every check in probes/checks.txt runs 3 times; any wrong status,
# missing text, slow response or wrong version fails the step.
set -uo pipefail
base="${1%/}"; sha="${2:-}"; header="${3:-}"; fail=0
args=(-s -o /tmp/probe-body -w '%{http_code} %{time_total}\n' --max-time 10)
[ -n "$header" ] && args+=(-H "$header")
[ -n "${PROBE_KEY:-}" ] && args+=(-H "X-API-Key: $PROBE_KEY")
summary="${GITHUB_STEP_SUMMARY:-/dev/stdout}"
echo "| Probe | Status | Time | Result |" >> "$summary"; echo "| --- | --- | --- | --- |" >> "$summary"

if [ -n "$sha" ]; then
  curl "${args[@]}" "$base/version" > /dev/null
  got=$(tr -d '[:space:]' < /tmp/probe-body)
  if [ "$got" = "$sha" ]; then echo "| GET /version | match | | pass |" >> "$summary"
  else echo "| GET /version | got '$got' | | FAIL |" >> "$summary"; fail=1; fi
fi

while read -r method path want text; do
  { [ -z "$method" ] || [ "${method:0:1}" = "#" ]; } && continue
  text="${text//\"/}"
  for _ in 1 2 3; do
    read -r code secs < <(curl "${args[@]}" -X "$method" "$base$path")
    ok=pass
    [ "$code" = "$want" ] || ok=FAIL
    if [ -n "$text" ] && ! grep -qF -- "$text" /tmp/probe-body; then ok=FAIL; fi
    awk "BEGIN { exit !($secs > ${MAX_SECONDS:-1.5}) }" && ok=FAIL
    echo "| $method $path | $code | ${secs}s | $ok |" >> "$summary"
    [ "$ok" = pass ] || fail=1
  done
done < probes/checks.txt
exit $fail

Turn on the workers.dev subdomain and preview URLs in your Wrangler config. Each uploaded version then gets its own URL, where it can be probed before any user reaches it.

The workflow first records the version that is live now. It then uploads the new one with no traffic, probes it at its preview URL, and moves 10% of traffic to it. During the canary, a version-override header makes sure the probes reach the new version. If the canary holds for three minutes, the workflow promotes the new version to 100% and probes production.         run: bash .github/factory/smoke.sh "$APP_URL" "$SHA"

      - name: Roll back and alert
        if: failure() && env.NEW != '' && env.STABLE != ''
        run: |
          npx wrangler versions deploy "$STABLE@100" --message "auto-rollback from $SHA" --yes
          gh issue create --title "Deploy rolled back: ${SHA:0:7}" --label factory-alert,escaped-bug \
            --assignee "${{ vars.ALERT_ASSIGNEE || github.repository_owner }}" \
            --body "Probes failed for $SHA, so production went back to version $STABLE. Run: ${{ github.server_url }}/${{ github.repository }}/actions/runs/${{ github.run_id }}"

Deploying to AWS Lambda instead

The Lambda version uses the same probes. It signs in to AWS with GitHub's OIDC tokens, so no AWS keys are stored anywhere. Create the function once with two aliases, live and candidate, and give each alias its own function URL. A new version goes to candidate first, gets probed there, and then receives 10% of live traffic through a weighted alias (AWS docs). The canary rounds probe live without a version check, because they may reach either version; the candidate probes have already checked the new one.

# .github/workflows/deploy-aws.yml  (use instead of deploy.yml)
name: deploy-aws
on:
  push:
    branches: [main]
  workflow_dispatch:

permissions:
  contents: read
  issues: write
  id-token: write          # lets the job sign in to AWS with OIDC

  group: deploy-production
  cancel-in-progress: false

    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    timeout-minutes: 20
    environment:
      name: production
      url: ${{ vars.APP_URL }}
    env:
      FN: ${{ vars.LAMBDA_FUNCTION }}
      APP_URL: ${{ vars.APP_URL }}               # function URL of the "live" alias
      CANDIDATE_URL: ${{ vars.CANDIDATE_URL }}   # function URL of the "candidate" alias
      PROBE_KEY: ${{ secrets.PROBE_API_KEY }}
      GH_TOKEN: ${{ secrets.GITHUB_TOKEN }}
      SHA: ${{ github.sha }}
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4

      - uses: aws-actions/configure-aws-credentials@v6
        with:
          role-to-assume: ${{ vars.AWS_DEPLOY_ROLE_ARN }}
          aws-region: ${{ vars.AWS_REGION }}

      - name: Build and test
        run: |
          make test
          make package                              # must produce dist/function.zip
          echo "$SHA" > VERSION && zip -j dist/function.zip VERSION   # /version serves this file

      - name: Publish a new version (no traffic yet)
        run: |
          echo "STABLE=$(aws lambda get-alias --function-name "$FN" --name live --query FunctionVersion --output text)" >> "$GITHUB_ENV"
          aws lambda update-function-code --function-name "$FN" --zip-file fileb://dist/function.zip > /dev/null
          aws lambda wait function-updated --function-name "$FN"
          NEW=$(aws lambda publish-version --function-name "$FN" --description "$SHA" --query Version --output text)
          echo "NEW=$NEW" >> "$GITHUB_ENV"
          aws lambda update-alias --function-name "$FN" --name candidate --function-version "$NEW" > /dev/null

      - name: Probe the new version before it takes traffic
        run: bash .github/factory/smoke.sh "$CANDIDATE_URL" "$SHA"

      - name: Canary at 10%
        run: |
          aws lambda update-alias --function-name "$FN" --name live --function-version "$STABLE" \
            --routing-config "{\"AdditionalVersionWeights\":{\"$NEW\":0.1}}" > /dev/null

      - name: Watch the canary for 3 minutes
        run: |
          for round in 1 2 3; do bash .github/factory/smoke.sh "$APP_URL" || exit 1; sleep 60; done

      - name: Promote to 100%
        run: |
          aws lambda update-alias --function-name "$FN" --name live --function-version "$NEW" \
            --routing-config '{"AdditionalVersionWeights":{}}' > /dev/null

      - name: Probe production
        run: bash .github/factory/smoke.sh "$APP_URL" "$SHA"

      - name: Roll back and alert
        if: failure() && env.STABLE != ''
        run: |
          aws lambda update-alias --function-name "$FN" --name live --function-version "$STABLE" \
            --routing-config '{"AdditionalVersionWeights":{}}'
          gh issue create --title "Deploy rolled back: ${SHA:0:7}" --label factory-alert,escaped-bug \
            --assignee "${{ vars.ALERT_ASSIGNEE || github.repository_owner }}" \
            --body "Probes failed for $SHA, so live went back to version $STABLE. Run: ${{ github.server_url }}/${{ github.repository }}/actions/runs/${{ github.run_id }}"

Guardrails and human checkpoints

The factory is only as trustworthy as checks the agent cannot see or change. StrongDM found its agents wrote return true to pass tests, which is why holdout scenarios exist (Stanford CodeX).

Agent edits its own rules Path guard fails any PR touching .github/, scenarios/ or AGENTS.md; rubric lives under .github/
Untrusted issue triggers a run Only people with triage access can apply factory:build; applying it is the human approval of the spec

Code or data leakage Free tiers may retain prompts for training; never put secrets or private data in the repo or specs

Where humans stay in the loop:

  • Write every spec with AI, then edit and approve it (applying the label is the sign-off).
  • Own the holdout scenarios repo; add a scenario for every escaped bug.
  • Resolve the spec red-team's comments before applying factory:build.
  • Clear the needs-human queue: medium/high risk PRs, reviewer rejects and failed runs.
  • Weekly audit: skim 5 auto-merged PRs and tighten AGENTS.md or the rubric when something slipped.
  • Label any bug found in auto-merged code escaped-bug, and act on the Factory alert issue when it opens.

For anything security-critical, keep the human merge on permanently; a public repo plus free models is a learning and side-project setup, not a production one.

Observability, alerts and evals

No one watches each run, so the factory has to report on itself, and people decide what each report means. Three loops do that for $0. Every run leaves a record. A nightly health check turns the records into metrics and alerts you when one drifts. Evals test the factory itself before any model, prompt or rubric changes.

What to watch

The health check computes these over the last 7 days from data GitHub already stores, so there is no database to run. The thresholds are starting points; tune them once you know your factory's normal range.

health.sh does the counting with gh and jq. The critical alerts can also stop the line: with an optional FACTORY_ADMIN_PAT (a fine-grained token with the repository's Variables permission set to read and write), it sets FACTORY_ENABLED=false, so no new builds start until a person looks.

bash
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# .github/factory/health.sh: 7-day factory metrics; opens, updates or closes one alert issue.
set -uo pipefail
since=$(date -u -d '7 days ago' +%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%SZ)

prs=$(gh pr list --label factory --state all --limit 200 --json number,createdAt,mergedAt,labels,body,comments \
  | jq --arg s "$since" '[.[] | select(.createdAt >= $s)]')
total=$(jq length <<<"$prs")
auto=$(jq '[.[] | select(.mergedAt != null and ([.labels[].name] | index("needs-human") | not))] | length' <<<"$prs")
human=$(jq '[.[] | select([.labels[].name] | index("needs-human"))] | length' <<<"$prs")
overturned=$(jq '[.[] | select(.mergedAt != null and ([.labels[].name] | index("needs-human")))] | length' <<<"$prs")
attempts=$(jq '[.[] | .body | capture("factory-run (?<j>\\{[^}]*\\})") | .j | fromjson | .attempts]
  | if length > 0 then (add / length * 10 | round / 10) else 0 end' <<<"$prs")
disagree=$(jq '[.[] | .comments[].body | capture("factory-review (?<j>\\{.*\\}) -->") | .j | fromjson
  | select(.prosecutor.approve != .second.approve)] | length' <<<"$prs")
failed=$(gh run list --workflow factory-build.yml --created ">=$since" --limit 200 --json conclusion \
  | jq '[.[] | select(.conclusion == "failure")] | length')
escaped=$(gh issue list --label escaped-bug --state all --search "created:>=${since%%T*}" --json number -q length)
left=$(curl -s https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/key -H "Authorization: Bearer $OPENROUTER_API_KEY" \
  | jq -r '.data.free_model_daily_requests.remaining // "unknown"')

alerts=()
[ "${HOLDOUTS:-}" = "failure" ] && alerts+=("CRITICAL: nightly holdouts failed against main")
[ "$escaped" -gt 0 ] && alerts+=("CRITICAL: $escaped escaped bug(s) reported this week")
if [ "$total" -ge 5 ]; then
  [ $((auto * 100 / total)) -lt 50 ] && alerts+=("First-pass success is $auto of $total PRs, below 50%")
  [ $((disagree * 100 / total)) -gt 30 ] && alerts+=("Reviewers disagreed on $disagree of $total PRs, above 30%")
fi
[ "$human" -ge 5 ] && [ $((overturned * 100 / human)) -gt 40 ] \
  && alerts+=("People merged $overturned of $human escalations: the reviewers may be too strict")
awk "BEGIN { exit !($attempts > 2.5) }" && alerts+=("Average attempts per spec is $attempts, above 2.5")
[ "$failed" -ge 3 ] && alerts+=("$failed build runs failed this week; check the logs for provider errors")

report=$(cat <<EOF
| Metric (last 7 days) | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Factory PRs opened | $total |
| Auto-merged with no person involved | $auto |
| Escalated to needs-human | $human (merged by a person: $overturned) |
| Average agent attempts per spec | $attempts |
| Reviewer disagreements | $disagree |
| Failed build runs | $failed |
| Escaped bugs reported | $escaped |
| Nightly holdouts on main | ${HOLDOUTS:-skipped} |
| Free OpenRouter requests left now | $left |
EOF
)
echo "$report" >> "$GITHUB_STEP_SUMMARY"

open=$(gh issue list --label factory-alert --state open --json number -q '.[0].number // empty')
if [ ${#alerts[@]} -gt 0 ]; then
  body="$(printf -- '- %s\n' "${alerts[@]}")"$'\n\n'"$report"
  if [ -n "$open" ]; then gh issue comment "$open" --body "$body"
  else gh issue create --title "Factory alert" --label factory-alert --assignee "$ASSIGNEE" --body "$body"; fi
  if [ -n "${DISCORD_WEBHOOK:-}" ]; then
    curl -s -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
      -d "$(jq -n --arg c "Factory alert: ${alerts[0]}" '{content: $c}')" "$DISCORD_WEBHOOK" > /dev/null
  fi
  if printf '%s\n' "${alerts[@]}" | grep -q '^CRITICAL' && [ -n "${FACTORY_ADMIN_PAT:-}" ]; then
    GH_TOKEN="$FACTORY_ADMIN_PAT" gh variable set FACTORY_ENABLED --body false && echo "Factory paused."
  fi
elif [ -n "$open" ]; then
  gh issue close "$open" --comment "All metrics are back inside their thresholds."$'\n\n'"$report"
fi

If your app isn't deployed anywhere, have run.sh build and start it from main before the scenarios run, the same way the verify job does. To resume after a pause, fix the cause, then set FACTORY_ENABLED back to true.

Evals: test the factory, not just the code

Tests check the software; evals check the machine that writes it. A free model can be swapped, withdrawn or quietly changed, and a rubric edit can make a reviewer blind. So keep three eval suites in the private scenarios repo, where the agents can't see them.

text

Reviewer evals Catch rate on diffs with a planted defect, and false rejects on clean diffs review.sh on every case, scored against expected.json 1 request per case per reviewer

Build your cases from real history. Each escaped bug becomes a seeded-defect case and a holdout scenario, and each spec that confused the agent becomes a golden spec. The suites then get harder in exactly the places your factory is weak.

The reviewer eval is a short script:

bash
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# evals/reviewer/run.sh <github|openrouter> <model> <rubric-file>  (run from the factory repo root)
caught=0; bugs=0; false_rejects=0; clean=0
for case in .holdout/evals/reviewer/cases/*/; do
  cp "$case/diff.patch" /tmp/diff.patch
  export SPEC="$(cat "$case/spec.md")"
  approve=$(bash .github/factory/review.sh "$1" "$2" "$3" | jq -r '.approve // false')
  if [ "$(jq -r .defect "$case/expected.json")" = "true" ]; then
    bugs=$((bugs + 1)); [ "$approve" != "true" ] && caught=$((caught + 1))
  else
    clean=$((clean + 1)); [ "$approve" != "true" ] && false_rejects=$((false_rejects + 1))
  fi
done
jq -n --argjson c "$caught" --argjson b "$bugs" --argjson f "$false_rejects" --argjson n "$clean" \
  '{catch_rate: ($c / $b), false_reject_rate: ($f / $n), cases: ($b + $n)}'

Costs. The health check uses one OpenRouter request and runner minutes. Twenty reviewer cases cost 20 requests per reviewer. Build evals are the expensive suite: on the 50-a-day tier, run them monthly or only before a model switch; with the $10 credit, weekly is comfortable.

Staying inside the free limits

The daily request cap, not Actions minutes, is what limits throughput. As a rough planning estimate (not a measured figure), one small spec costs 5–15 model requests across build attempts and test-fix turns, plus 2 for the adversarial reviews and 1 for the spec red-team.

No credit ever bought 50 3–4
$10 bought once 1,000 60+ (then the 20/min cap and runner time dominate)

Adding Mistral, Groq and Gemini to the chain and moving review to GitHub Models adds separate daily quotas on top of these figures.

Tactics that stretch the budget:

  • Small specs. One acceptance criterion cluster per issue; split anything over ~300 lines of change.
  • Small context. --map-tokens 1024 and listing likely files in the spec keep each request cheap and focused.
  • Quota check first. The build job reads GET /api/v1/key and stops early rather than burning failed requests, which still count.
  • Nightly queue drain. Add a schedule: trigger at 00:30 UTC that applies factory:build to the oldest factory:queued issues, so each day's fresh quota goes to work while you sleep.
  • Fallback chain. Spread the implementer across providers with FACTORY_MODELS; free endpoints get removed with little warning, so a 404 should fall through, not fail the run.
  • Mind concurrency. GitHub keeps only one pending run per concurrency group and cancels older pending ones, so labelling ten issues at once leaves eight cancelled; use the nightly queue instead.
  • Public repo. Keeps Actions free and unmetered; a private repo works but every agent loop eats into 2,000 minutes a month.

Turning the lights off gradually

Start with every merge manual and widen auto-merge only as the holdout pass rate earns it.

  1. Week 1 — Assisted. The factory opens PRs; you merge all of them. Remove the gh pr merge line until reviewer verdicts match your own judgment most of the time.
  2. Weeks 2–3 — Docs and tests auto-merge. Allow auto-merge only when the diff touches docs/ or tests/.
  3. Week 4+ — Low-risk auto-merge. Enable the workflow as written: green tests, green holdouts, reviewer approve and risk=low.
  4. Steady state — Dark. Nightly queue drain. People spend their time on the judgement work: specs, hidden scenarios, the needs-human queue and the factory's own standards.

Move right only when the gate holds: the reviewer agrees with you on most PRs, then the holdout pass rate stays steady, then the weekly audit of auto-merged PRs finds nothing that slipped. Step back a phase whenever a gate breaks. The health check makes each gate measurable: reviewer disagreement and escalations merged by a person for the first, nightly holdout results for the second, and escaped bugs for the third.

Cheapest upgrades when free stops being enough:

  • One-time $10 OpenRouter credit: 20× the daily cap, no ongoing cost.
  • Pay only for the reviewer: a cheap paid model as REVIEW_MODEL buys the most trust per dollar, since review is 1–2 requests per PR.
  • Swap the agent: aider can be replaced by OpenCode, which ships OpenRouter as a built-in provider (Pinggy); the workflow shape stays the same.
  • Self-hosted runner: no longer free in private repos, which carry a $0.002/min platform charge since March 2026 (dev.to).

Caveats. Free models rotate monthly and vary in quality; expect to re-pick models every few weeks. Free-tier terms may allow prompt retention, so keep this to open-source or non-sensitive code. And the core question stays open even for well-funded teams: how to prove software works when agents wrote both the code and its tests. Holdouts are the best cheap answer.
